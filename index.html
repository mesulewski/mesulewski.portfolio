As Learning Quality Expert, I applied the Academy’s quality assurance and learning recognition frameworks across a library targeting 50–80 new and migrated courses a year, on a platform available in 12 languages to 7,000+ learners.

I advised learning designers, production and technology teams, and WHO technical units on instructional design, assessment and learning experience standards; organized the biannual Quality Committee; and designed training workshops for colleagues and external partners.

The human side of quality

Quality review means reviewing colleagues’ work, and sometimes telling them it isn’t ready yet. That’s where most of the job really happens. Over the years, I’ve learned to make that feedback land: anchor every comment in a shared standard rather than personal taste, say what works before what doesn’t, propose an action rather than just naming a problem, and stay open when a designer pushes back with a good reason.

That’s why the checklist above has a column for the designer’s response: a review is a conversation, not a verdict. The same skills carried into onboarding new QA colleagues and running training workshops for designers and partners, where the goal is for teams to own quality themselves.

Teaching an AI pipeline what a learning experience is for

I turned an internal generative AI tool that produced plausible but aimless questions into a pipeline that designs learning experiences backward from the objective.

Role
Senior Learning Designer, pedagogical lead on the tool
Organization
A European language-learning platform (exam prep, general and business paths, 60,000+ monthly active users)
Team
Engineering built the tool; I owned its pedagogy
I owned
Learning outcome rules, item-writing rules, quality-check rules, and the requirements for a prompt-testing playground
Frameworks
Backward design, CEFR, form-meaning connections, input processing, the DAPA sequence (Discover, Assimilate, Practice, Action)

The problem

The first version of the tool could generate questions, but not the questions a learning designer needed. Testing it, I wrote:

“I need to be able to tell the tool what kind of learning experience I want to create, and for it to understand the main point of that experience.”
My notes from early testing

Changing the target skill while keeping the same text produced near-identical questions. Vocabulary was a required input even when vocabulary wasn’t the point. Some items had no single correct answer, options drifted beyond the target scope, and complex grammar came out wrong. Adding more input didn’t help.

How it works now

Each activity is built in sequence from its objective, then screened before it reaches learners. Open any step to see the rule behind it.

  1. The designer starts from what the learner should be able to do, for example “express opinions and preferences” at B1. Not from a vocabulary list.
  2. Each outcome is observable, stays within the target CEFR level, and decomposes the objective faithfully. One rule captures the idea: the theme is the setting, not the skill.
  3. The story is built to carry the objective, with enough stakes to be worth reading even at low levels.
  4. Answers must be stated explicitly in the text and tested by paraphrase, never by keyword matching. Distractors follow defined types: keyword traps and scope traps. The correct answer is never the longest option, and no question gives away another’s answer.
  5. An AI judge screens items against named criteria before anything is published. Designers test prompt changes in a playground I specified, without touching production.

What learners see

Key decisions

Generate from the objective, one step at a time

I restructured generation as a chain of decisions, from objective to outcomes to story to questions.

Rejected · Adding more input fields. The early build already asked for too much and still missed the point.

Write item-writing standards into the prompts

The rules a trained assessment writer follows, made explicit and testable: grounding, paraphrase, distractor types, no test-wise cues, independent items.

Rejected · Trusting the model’s default judgment and fixing items by hand. At scale, manual correction becomes the bottleneck the tool was meant to remove.

Organize around activity types, with checks built in

A discovery story, a grammar-noticing task and a practice set need different items, so generation runs per DAPA activity type, with a rule-based judge and a prompt playground around it.

Rejected · One general-purpose question generator for every context.

Outcomes

Discover activities are live for General English and General French learners.

367activities generated
~61 hof learning content
~1 hto generate them, vs. days to weeks by hand
582learners
91.5%of 11,783 activities started were completed
90%of completed activities scored 60% or more

What I learned

A model follows explicit rules, not intentions. The standards an expert applies without thinking had to be written down, one testable rule at a time.

Sequence matters more than detail. Breaking design into ordered decisions did more for quality than any added input.

Designers need a safe place to experiment. The playground turned prompt changes from a developer request into a design task.

AI conversation practice: from speaking tasks to real conversations

I co-designed an AI conversation feature where learners practice real situations with a character who reacts and pushes back, then get feedback on whether their message landed.

Role
Senior Learning Designer; I owned the AI’s conversational behavior, the feedback pipeline, the evaluation criteria and the overall learning experience
Team
Product owner, UX designers, front- and back-end engineers
Timeline
Design sprint in January 2026, then build and launch
Frameworks
Task-based language teaching, intelligibility, corrective feedback research, the affective filter

The problem

The platform’s existing AI speaking activity was a monologue: learners recorded an answer and got a score. In a five-workshop design sprint, our team did the activity as learners and logged every friction. There was no one to talk to. Feedback focused on vocabulary and told learners their opinion was the “wrong answer.” Learners had to use target words even when they could say the same thing more naturally, and they had no sense of progress.

A review of competitors showed the same gap: none offered genuinely qualitative feedback on how well a learner communicated.

Key decisions

Evaluate the mission, then whatever most gets in the way of understanding

Four criteria, with intelligibility as the compass rather than a fifth criterion. Pick one to see how it’s used:

Rejected · Grading accuracy on target vocabulary and grammar. Fluency and sociolinguistic appropriateness were also left out for now: fluency is too tied to pronunciation, which is still being explored.

Give the AI a character with a stake, and protect the learner’s confidence

The partner has their own opinion and pushes back, so learners must react as in real life. But it never interrupts to correct.

Rejected · Mid-conversation corrections. I flagged that error-focused interruptions raise the affective filter and stop learners from speaking at all.

Three fixed steps on very few criteria

Signal on task achievement, then Hint and Model on the same single criterion. The order is part of the pedagogy: reversing Hint and Model would cancel the learner’s effort.

Rejected · Covering every criterion at once, a binary verdict, and techniques that ask the learner to speak again, which belong in the conversation, not at the end.

Repetition without redundancy

The same communication skills recur across different contexts, characters and stakes, and learners can ask for a custom challenge.

Outcomes

90%+learner satisfaction
1,000+responses
~80challenges in five categories

What I learned

Interaction changes what you can assess. Once there’s a listener, intelligibility becomes measurable in a way a monologue never allows.

Output isn’t enough. Learners acquire language by hearing it, so the character’s speech is part of the lesson, not just a prompt.

Assessing our assessments

At OpenClassrooms, I helped build a review process that audited how project-based assessments were actually conducted, then turned the findings into fixes owned by three different teams.

Role
Learning QA Manager on a seven-person Learning QA team; I led the rollout on the Data learning paths
Organization
OpenClassrooms, an online school with diploma-granting, project-based paths (600+ courses, 40,000–52,000 learners a year)
Timeline
A two-month pilot, then a rolling rollout through 2022
Partners
Subject matter expert reviewers, Mentorship, Product, Learning Design

The problem

There are no exams at OpenClassrooms. Students complete job-like projects and defend them live to an assessor, who validates the project or sends it back. That model puts a great deal of weight on each assessor’s judgment, and our only quality signal was reactive: tickets.

The pilot answered our question clearly. The model was sound, but criteria were applied inconsistently, and even our own reviewers didn’t always agree with each other. Six expert reviewers audited 145 assessments against a target of 100: 15% of all assessments in the period, every one of the path’s 14 projects, and 36% of active assessors.

What we audited

Six parts of every assessment, from the brief to the verdict.

The review cycle

  1. Random sampleRecent assessments, assigned monthly
  2. ReviewTwo expert reviewers, one standard form
  3. CalibrationReviewers and QA specialist compare decisions monthly
  4. Quarterly reportFindings by path, project and assessor

MentorshipPath-specific assessor training; escalation
ProductDeliverable-check reminders, partial report automation

Key decisions

Audit the whole assessment, not just the verdict

Rejected · Re-grading final decisions only. That shows that assessors disagree, but not why.

Prototype two review depths and pilot before scaling

A light and a long review on one standard form, tested on a single path, to find the detail that gave useful data and could still run everywhere.

Rejected · Launching on all paths at once.

Calibrate the reviewers, not just the assessors

Every rollout started with an interview, an onboarding session and a calibration exercise; each month, reviewers and a QA specialist compared decisions.

Route each finding to the team that owns the fix

Rejected · Treating every finding as a content update.

Outcomes

−19%evaluator discrepancies on the Data paths in six months
100%of diploma paths with 50+ students covered by Q3 2022
145assessments audited in the pilot

The 19% compares validation outcomes on the reworked projects before and after the rework. I wrote up the project in a two-part article.

What I learned

Consistency is a property of the system. Fixing it took clearer projects, better training and platform changes together.

Calibration is a habit, not a step. A one-time alignment drifts; a monthly comparison keeps reviewers honest with each other.

Learning experiences

How I design learning, one pedagogical job at a time: learners first meet the content in a form they can understand, then notice the pattern and build the rule themselves, then use it with less and less support. The examples below come from language learning, but the sequence carries to any skill. Every activity is playable.

1 · InputUnderstand a real example first. The concept arrives as meaning, not as a rule.Beyond languages: a patient case before the protocol, a worked analysis before the formula. 2 · NoticingFind the pattern, work with it, and build the rule from your own answers.Beyond languages: comparing two code snippets to work out why one fails. 3 · OutputApply it, first with support, then in a realistic task with feedback.Beyond languages: a role-played client call, judged on whether the message landed.

Most examples come from one B2 English lesson on the modal perfect (must / might / should have + past participle): Koffi gets home after a long trip and can’t find his suitcase. The full lesson is at the end of the page.

1 · Input: understanding comes first

Learners acquire language they understand. Input activities remove what would block comprehension, give learners a reason to listen, and deliver the target form inside a story with stakes, before any rule.

2 · Noticing: from meaning to form

Learners can’t acquire a form they never notice. These activities draw attention to the form inside the story, make learners process it to complete a task, and let them build the rule themselves.

The same principle at A1 and C1

A1–A2 · Discovery before explanation

Clinic assistant: check the records

From Samira’s Sick Day. Learners choose has or feels for each symptom; the rule appears only after they’ve tried.

B2–C1 · Co-constructing a scale

Which planner sounds less certain?

From A City at a Crossroads. Three comparisons, and the certainty scale is built from the learner’s answers.

3 · Output: using the form to communicate

Producing the form makes learners notice what they can’t yet say. Output starts guided, with support that fades, and ends in a real exchange where the form is needed to move the conversation forward.

Feedback on output: signal, hint, model

When learners produce language, feedback moves in three fixed steps: a signal on whether the message landed, a hint on the one thing that most hurts intelligibility, and a model only after the learner has had a chance to repair.

A B1 learner calls a hotel to report a problem. Reveal the feedback one step at a time:

“The thing that is supposed to make the room warm is not doing what it should do since this morning.”

SignalYou got your message across. Your listener just had to work for it.
HintYou’re describing the object and its problem in a roundabout way. Try naming both directly.
Model“The heater hasn’t been working since this morning.” The object and the problem, named in a few words. The listener understands immediately.
